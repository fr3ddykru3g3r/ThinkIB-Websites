  • 3b. The Bolshevik Revolution of 1917

The Provisional Government had taken control in March 1917.  However, this regime failed to stabilise the situation in Russia and its authority was undermined by having to share power with the Petrograd Soviet.  By October/November 1917 Lenin, believed that the time had come for the Bolsheviks to seize control.

Guiding questions:

How effective was the Provisional Government?

Why were the Bolsheviks able to seize power in October 1917?

How effective was the Provisional Government?

What was the Provisional Government?

On 2 March a Provisional Government was declared, made up largely of leading figures of various liberal parties but dominated by the Kadets. The Provisional Government met in the Tauride Palace.

However, in another wing of the palace, the 'Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies' was formed. This became the focus for workers and soldiers' interests. It controlled the railways, telegraph station, factories, power supplies and the soldiers in the Petrograd garrison.

The Kadets, under the leadership of Paul Milyukov, had support mainly from the middle classes and wanted a democratically elected assembly, civil rights for all classes, legitimacy of trade unions, free access to education and the end of state censorship.  There had been divisions within the party before the revolution as the right-wing supported the retention of the monarchy whereas the left wanted a full republic. 

The first prime minister of the Provisional Government was Prince Lvov. 

The government was called 'provisional' as its aim was to temporarily govern until elections for a Constituent Assembly were held. 

For the first two months of the Revolution, there was harmony between these two groups in what became known as the 'honeymoon' period. Several key measures were taken such as:

  • arrest and imprisonment of Tsarist ministers and officials

  • the disbanding of the secret police

  • amnesty for political and religious prisoners

  • the establishment of the freedom of the press and of free speech

  • abolition of the death penalty

  • agreement to call elections for a new Constituent Assembly that would determine the new government of Russia; these elections would be help by secret ballot and universal suffrage

What challenges did the Provisional Government face?

The Provisional Government seemed to operate quite successfully in the first two months after the fall of the Tsar.  Its initial measures (see above) had public support and it did not get into conflict with the Petrograd Soviet.   Soldiers in the capital were content that they had not been sent to the front lines and the workers had been given the right to strike and better working conditions. 

The Tsarist ministers across Russia were removed and the local councils (zemstvo) took over, however these mustered little support or respect.  Committees were established of workers and soldiers but these were overtaken by the formation of soviet organisations.  In the countryside, peasants also started to set up committees for their own representation.  Prince Lvov not only tolerated these - but encouraged local bodies to run their own affairs.

However, the Provisional Government faced several key problems which it was unable to resolve and in April, Lenin returned to Petrograd determined to capitalise on these problems and to force a second revolution.

The Petrograd Soviet and Dual Power

Five days before the Provisional Government met, another organisation was established in the very same building - the Petrograd Soviet.  This became the key authority for the working classes as it called for factories to elect delegates which then chose an Executive Committee.  Soldiers demanded to be represented in the Soviet on 1st March and this led to the Soviet issuing Order No.1. 

Order No.1 was important as it gave representation to soldiers and subsequently weapons were put under the control of their committees.  Significantly, the order declared that soldiers would only follow the orders of the Provisional Government if the Soviet agreed. 

The Petrograd Soviet became the 'Soviet of Workers' and Soldier' Deputies' and was initially politically dominated by Mensheviks.

The power of the Soviet led to a division of authority in Russia known as 'dual power'.  Indeed, in reality, the Provisional Government could not govern without the backing of the Soviet. 

  • The Provisional Government was unelected and therefore did not have a clear mandate to rule
  • The Provisional Government was dominated by the middles classes
  • The Soviet represented the workers and soldiers
  • The Soviet controlled the factories
  • The Soviet controlled the power supplies
  • The Soviet controlled the railways and communications
  • The Soviet had control over the soldiers in the Petrograd garrison

There was one socialist member of the Provisional Government - Alexander Kerensky and he was Minister of Justice. But Kerensky was also vice-chairman of the Petrograd Soviet.  He would later play a key role in the events that led to the Bolshevik revolution (see below).

Although obviously powerful, the Soviet did not move to form a government to replace the Provisional Government as its leadership did not believe that the time was right for a working class revolution.  The leadership also believed that an attempt to overthrow the government at this point would lead to a civil war;  military commanders had kept troops away from the capital on the proviso that the Duma retained control. There was also a fear that the 'masses' could not be kept under control by the Soviet leadership if the Provisional Government was toppled.

Commitment to the First World War

But another key issue for the Provisional Government was the war.  In April a crisis developed as the minister for war, Milyukov, wanted to go beyond the defensive strategy the Soviets had backed and to make territorial gains.  He was forced to resign.  At the beginning of May, five socialists were brought into the government, including leaders of the Menshevik and Socialist Revolutionary parties.  The government then confirmed it would be fighting only a defensive war. 

However, both the Mensheviks and the SRs were from this point tied to Russia's increasingly unpopular commitment to the war.

Then, in June 1917 the government launched an offensive against the Germans, not only because of pressure from Britain and France to act but also because the Kadets and nationalists did not want to be humiliated by Germany and wanted the conservative forces to regain influence in the government.  Even some socialists who wanted peace supported an offensive as it would put Russia in a better negotiating position for peace terms.  The new minister of war was Kerensky. 

He was generally popular, and he toured the nation and front lines making speeches in an attempt to rally support and boost morale behind the offensive.  But Kerensky found opposition from soldiers committees who did not want to fight when there seemed little to be gained.  Thousands of troops deserted the front before the offensive began on 16 June.  Hundreds of thousands were then killed in the offensive and some soldiers mutinied and shot their officers.  The Germans took more territory and this military disaster led to revolt in Petrograd in July. 

The moderate socialists lost all credibility due to association with the war effort.  This in turn led to more support for the Bolshevik agenda (see below).

The economic situation

The economic situation did not improve and food and fuel shortages were acute.  The workers were badly impacted and due to further deterioration of transport and supply routes, factories cut production and workers were laid off.  By July 1917, 568 factories in Petrograd alone had closed down.  Inflation continued unabated and the grain crisis had become critical.  The harvest was bad in 1917 and the government's offer to pay the peasants a 100% increase did not improve supply. 

Brigades were sent to the countryside to requisition grain - but this just increased hostility towards the government.  Due to the war, food had to be prioritised for the army and this led to even more tension in the cities.  There were widespread strikes and workers committees took over control of some factories.  Wages did not keep up with inflation - with bread prices rising by 500% and meat by nearly 800% between August 1914 and August 1917.  Fuel was also unaffordable - firewood had increased by over 1000% in this period.

The issue of land

The peasants wanted land, however the liberals in the government wanted to wait until an elected government could determine its redistribution.  When the socialists joined the government in May the peasants hoped that the land issue would be addressed - but in general socialist ministers agreed that the matter had to wait for the elected Constituent Assembly. 

Throughout June and July peasants began to seize land for themselves.  The SRs lost credit with the peasants for not pushing land reform through the government.

The national minorities

The national minorities were also demanding independence - Poles and Fins, and the important economic territory - 'the bread basket of Russia' - Ukraine called for self-government.  However the government did not want to see the empire break up, and after concessions were given to Ukraine, nationalists were outraged and three Kadet ministers resigned in July.

What was the role of Kerensky?

In July 1917, Kerensky was made prime minister as he was seen as the only man that would be able to 'save Russia' and unite the country.

He wanted to keep the liberals, Kadets in a coalition government with the socialists - although the workers and peasants were demanding radical solutions. 

He was charismatic and intelligent, but also an egotist who promoted his own image in government propaganda.  He moved into the Winter palace and kept on the Tsars old servants.

Kerensky faced very serious issues - the army was falling apart.  Soldiers were taking over trains and there were mass desertions and violence.  The food crisis had intensified and in the countryside land seizures were escalating and estates were burnt down and looted.  Across the empire crime was on the rise. 

The Soviet's influence was decreasing as the workers and soldiers became disillusioned with it.  The liberals in the government now wanted firmer control and shifted to the right - demanding military discipline and law and order.  At the same time popular support for the Bolsheviks was increasing.

You will see below that Kerensky then made key decisions during the late summer and autumn of 1917 that would foster the Bolshevik seizure of power in October/November 1917.

The growing power of the Bolsheviks

As you have discovered in working through the material and activities above, the Provisional Government was compromised from its inception by the alternative authority of the Petrograd Soviet.  It was also undermined by the fact it was an unelected body and had decided to continue to fight the First World War.

The return of Lenin

Lenin returned to Russia, with the assistance of the German government in a sealed railway carriage in April 1917.  He then made a key speech, based on his 'April Theses' drafted on his journey, to the waiting crowd at the Finland station in Petrograd. In the speech he called for the Soviet to take power, land to be given to the peasants, an end to cooperation with the Provisional Government and an immediate end to the war.

At a meeting of the Social Democrats, the Mensheviks condemned the ideas in Lenin's 'theses' as not being in line with Marxist doctrine and even some leading Bolsheviks thought he had lost touch with reality whilst in exile.

However, by the beginning of May, Lenin's Theses had become the accepted policy of the Bolsheviks.  The strength of his personality and political argument had won over the party.  He argued that the struggle between the workers and the bourgeoisie was more acute in Russia than in the more industrially advanced nations and that this created the right conditions for global socialist revolution to start there.  Russia's revolution would then spark revolutions by workers across Europe - Lenin and Leon Trotsky believed the next revolution would be in Germany.

The slogans of 'All power to the Soviets' and 'Bread, Peace and Land' were now promoted and had widespread appeal - particularly for workers, soldiers and peasants.

During the summer of 1917 workers and soldiers in Petrograd were increasingly drawn to the Bolsheviks.  Leftist SRs and Mensheviks also moved to support the Bolshevik party. In addition, the high profile revolutionary Leon Trotsky joined the party.

July Days and the Kornilov Revolt

The failure of the summer offensive, the continuing economic crisis and the fear by its garrison troops that they would be sent to the front led to protests and riots on the streets of Petrograd in July - the July Days.

On 4th July, sailors from the Kronstadt naval base joined the demonstrations.  The sailors marched to the Tauride palace and demanded that the Soviet take control.

The rising had been encouraged by some Bolsheviks but the leadership had not backed it.  Lenin appealed for restraint and gave a confusing speech to the sailors and protestors.  He did not want to lead 'the mob' as the rising had not been organised and Lenin was concerned the protestors could not be controlled.

The crowds were then dispersed by loyal troops.  The Provisional Government leaked a letter that suggested Lenin was an agent in the pay of the Germans.  He fled into hiding in Finland.

The Soviet newspaper Ivestia condemned the role of the Bolsheviks in the uprising and leading Bolsheviks were arrested.  Its newspaper was closed down and it seemed at the time that this could be the end of the party. 

By August Kerensky faced multiple pressures and crises; commitment to the war was increasingly unpopular but he did not want to sign a separate peace with Germany at huge cost to Russia.  This also meant that he was unable to redress the dire economic situation, nor contain the breakdown of law and order.  In addition, he resisted cracking down too hard on the Bolsheviks as he believed this could lead to more unrest.

Kerensky appointed a new commander in chief of the armed forces - General Kornilov.  However, Kornilov decided to take matters into his own hands and to send his troops to the capital to restore order and crush the radicals.  Kerensky then panicked as he saw this as a move by Kornilov to put the military in control.  He called for help from the Soviet to defend against this 'counter-revolution' and supplied them with weapons.

Solders, sailors and workers prepared to stop Kornilov's forces, with the Bolsheviks doing much of the organisation.  Trotsky was released from prison and Bolshevik Red Guards took to the streets.  Railway workers stopped the troop trains and Bolshevik agents persuaded his Kornilov's men to abandon the plan.  Hundreds of officers were murdered by soldiers who deemed the affair an 'officers plot'.  Kornilov himself was arrested.

Kerensky's, and his associates the Mensheviks and SRs, credibility was severely damaged.  But, the Bolsheviks were seen as the heroes and saviours of the revolution

At the beginning of September the Bolsheviks gained control of the Petrograd Soviet and by the end of the month Trotsky was elected president.

The Kornilov affair had created a 'power vacuum' which Lenin now seized on - he returned to Petrograd, and on 10 October persuaded his party to plan a seizure of power.

The Bolsheviks set up the Military Revolutionary Committee (MRC) which had control over soldiers in the capital, these soldiers then seized arms and ammunition.  Kerensky attempted to move against the Bolsheviks but Lenin claimed he was attacking the Soviet. 

Kerensky would later flee the capital.

The Bolshevik Revolution October/November 1917

Trotsky and Yakov Sverdlov (Bolshevik party administrator and chairman of the All-Russian Central Executive Committee) organised the details for the execution of the revolution from the Smolny institute.

During the night of 24-25th October Red Guard units seized key infrastructure points - telephones, railway and power stations and bridges.

The epic 'storming of Winter Palace' was a myth later promoted by the Bolsheviks - in reality there was little resistance

The following events then took place:

  • The All Russian Congress of Soviets met the same evening.  Other socialist parties condemned the Bolsheviks actions
  • Trotsky declared that 'your role is played out; go where you ought to be - into the dustbin of history!'
  • Key socialist parties stormed out in protest. This left the Bolsheviks in the majority in the Congress
  • Lenin arrived and announced the formation of a Bolshevik government and that moves would be begin to end the war and give land to the peasants.

There was limited violence in Petrograd - but it took 10 days of force to gain control in Moscow

What was Lenin's role in the revolution?

Vladmimir Illich Ulyanov - Lenin - declared himself a Marxist in 1889

He was arrested and sent into exile in Siberia for Marxist activities, and later travelled to Europe to meet with other Marxists.

Lenin became leader of the Bolsheviks in 1903.

When the First World War broke out Lenin was in exile in Switzerland.  He had advocated for Russian defeat in the First World War arguing it would hasten revolution.  After the February/March revolution had overthrown the Tsar, he returned to Russia (with German assistance) in April 1917.

  • He proclaimed his April Theses - together with popular slogans - All power to the Soviets, Peace and Land
  • Lenin had called the political situation regarding the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet 'Dual Power'
  • He called for the workers, peasants and soldiers not to be 'deceived' by the government and to take power for the Soviet
  • It was Lenin that spent weeks persuading other party members to adopt his agenda - he succeeded and the Bolshevik party endorsed it at its April Conference.
  • He argued that a new Soviet government would begin peace negotiations immediately, nationalise all land and redistribute it to the peasants and take control of privately owned industry to ensure workers benefitted from their labour.
  • By September Lenin had made the Bolsheviks the majority group in the Petrograd Soviet
  • He urged the party to prepare for an armed rising to overthrow the Provisional Government throughout September in articles sent from hiding in Finland.

On 20 October Lenin, in disguise, returned to Petrograd and then attended a meeting of the Bolshevik Central Committee on 23rd October.

He engaged in a 10-hour debate to win the majority over to an armed seizure of power.

He was assisted by Trotsky who planned out the details as he had the strategic position as leader in the Petrograd Soviet.

Lenin, this time in hiding in Petrograd, again urged for action on evening 6th November.  On the night of 7th-8th November Bolshevik Red Guards arrested the remnants of the Provisional Government.

Lenin himself declared that power was now in the hands of the Soviets.

How significant was Trotsky's role in the revolution?

  • Trotsky became Chairman of the Petrograd Soviet in September 1917
  • Bolsheviks dominated the Soviets in Moscow and other industrial centres as well
  • He formed the Military Revolutionary Committee to help prepare seizure of power
  • Trotsky formed Red Guard to storm Winter Palace
  • He planned the details of the seizure of power
  • Trotsky was an intellectual, effective leader and charismatic speaker

Was the Bolshevik revolution a coup d'etat?

The Bolshevik propaganda after 1917 claimed that the October revolution had been a popular rising carried out by the workers (via the soviets and supported by the Bolshevik Party) and supported by the peasants. Lenin was the key figure who guided the party and directed the Revolution. This line was maintained by Soviet historians up to 1991.

Conversely, western historians in the atmosphere of the Cold War saw the October revolution as a coup d’état carried out by a small group of Bolsheviks. They were tightly controlled and directed by Lenin who then went on to lay the foundations of the totalitarianism of Stalin.

Other Western historians challenged this view in the 1980s claiming that the lower ranks of the Bolshevik party were also in the October revolution, that indeed it was more of a popular revolution. More recently, most historians agree that the revolution was a coup d’état in the way that power was seized but that in fact the workers had become increasingly radicalised throughout 1917 and had played a role in laying the foundations for the Bolshevik takeover. Lenin’s determination and persistence however was still key for ensuring that the revolution actually took place.
