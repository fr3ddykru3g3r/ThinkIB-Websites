Sorting out sentence structure

Students use sentences. Perhaps they are correct. They are short. They are not very interesting. They do not sound clever. The student may be more clever. The ideas are not linked.

Alternatively... We all know the student who goes on and on, he has another idea, he doesn't use anything apart from commas, this is not very impressive either, sentence structure matters I think, it is because the grammatical structure of sentences is the structure of thought, the student who goes on like this with barely a cohesive device in sight, he is not very clear, the examiner will not be pleased ... (it hurts to write this!)

I propose that students with a weak grasp of sentence structure need to be given a simple toolkit of ways to link ideas together. This toolkit will need to be explained, and examples provided.

You can either use the basic handout provided below; or you can project the material using Presentation mode.

Sentence structure toolkit

Simple

Parenthesis :- brackets / dashes / pairs of commas

Adding details with prepositions

Using ‘to’

Using ‘-ing’ and ‘-ed’

Adding lists - to the Subject, the Verb, and the Object

Complex

.

Using punctuation or linkers

Using relative pronouns / ‘question words’

Framing with ‘reported’ structures

Handout

Sentence Structure: Essentials, summarising this page

*****

Simple

Peter played computer games

Parenthesis :- brackets / dashes / pairs of commas

Peter (the computer wizard) played computer games

Peter - the computer wizard - played computer games

Peter, the computer wizard, played computer games

Adding details with prepositions

In his room, at home, Peter played computer games

Peter played computer games on his laptop in class

Using ‘to’

Peter played computer games to develop his programming skills

Using ‘-ing’ and ‘-ed’

Peter, muttering to himself, played computer games

+

Peter, tired of homework, played computer games

Tired of homework, Peter played computer games

Adding lists - to the Subject, the Verb, and the Object

Peter, Anna and many others played computer games

Peter bought, learned and played computer games

Peter played computer games, poker and chess

*****

.

When the student has properly grasped the idea of adding elements on to the core sentence ('Peter played computer games'), we can move on to more sophisticated ways of linking ideas together in a complex sentence:-

Complex

eat to live ... live to eat

Using punctuation or linkers

Some people eat to live ; others live to eat

Don’t eat to live - live to eat

I don’t eat to live, but rather live to eat

Using relative pronouns / ‘question words’

People who have a practical view of things eat to live, while those who have a more sensual view live to eat

OR

People who eat to live have a practical view of things, while those who live to eat have a more sensual view

People eat to live when they are under pressure, but live to eat when they have the time

OR

While people are under pressure, they eat to live, but when they have the time, they live to eat

OR

People who are under pressure eat to live, whereas people who have the time live to eat

OR

People who are under pressure, whatever type of pressure it may be, eat to live, whereas people who have the time live to eat, provided that they have the money

Framing with ‘reported’ structures

It is generally accepted that some people eat to live and others live to eat

OR

Some people say that they eat to live ; others that they live to eat

*****

.

Blending simple and complex

Finally, one can look at the way in which Simple and Complex techniques can be mixed together :-

Anna decided to study Economics

Parenthesis :- brackets / dashes / pairs of commas

Anna - an ambitious girl - decided to study Economics.

Adding details with prepositions

In May, Anna - an ambitious girl - decided to study Economics at Cambridge.

Using ‘to’

In March, Anna - an ambitious girl - decided to study Economics at Cambridge to improve her prospects of a job.

Using ‘-ing’ and ‘-ed’

Finishing her course in May, Anna - an ambitious girl, committed to her career - decided to study Economics at Cambridge to improve her prospects of a job.

Adding lists - to the Subject, the Verb, and the Object

Finishing her course (classes, revision and exams) in May, Anna - an ambitious girl, committed to her career - did some research, took advice and decided to study Economics at Cambridge to improve her prospects of a job, a good salary, and a chance to travel.

Using relative pronouns / ‘question words'

Finishing her course (classes, revision and exams) in May, Anna - an ambitious girl, committed to her career - did some research, which took some time, took advice wherever she could get it, and decided to study Economics at Cambridge to improve her prospects of a job, a good salary, and a chance to travel when she felt like it.

Framing with ‘reported’ structures (notice tense changes)

We were told that, having finished her course (classes, revision and exams) in May, Anna - an ambitious girl, committed to her career - had done some research, which took some time, had taken advice wherever she could get it, and had decided to study Economics at Cambridge to improve her prospects of a job, a good salary, and a chance to travel when she felt like it.

*****
