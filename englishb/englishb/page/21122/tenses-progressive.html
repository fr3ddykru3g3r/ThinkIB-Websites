Tenses progressive

  • /
  • Language System/
  • Key Language Issues/
  • Tenses, explored/
  • Tenses progressive

We call tenses 'progressive' (or 'continuous' according to some grammar books) if they include the '-ing' structure. 

Such progressive tenses exist to indicate an action which was happening at the same time as another action occurred. They could be seen as 'background' tenses, which provide a context for 'foreground' tenses. This means that they are very often used with the word 'when' to link the two verbs together - e.g. "I was walking down the street when I saw Maria."

When you have reviewed the various tenses explained below, try the Practice Exercise.

Present

For example:

"I'm really glad you called, because I am talking to Laura right now - so I can pass on the message."

The past-to-present

For example:

"I have been thinking of calling you all morning, but I haven't had time..."

Definitely past

For example:

"I was living in California when I first heard about the IB."

Past-beyond-the-past

For example:

"My family had been living in Indonesia for four years when my mother was offered a job in Japan."

.

Practice exercise

> Run through the following questions to see how well you can choose the right progressive tense.

Note that this is a 'dynamic quiz' - the questions change every time you open this page!

He ......................... for three hours when the bus finally arrived.

The waiting is continuous up to the moment when the bus arrived - the past tense of "arrived" means that the verb 'wait' has to be one step further back in the past: the 'had + ing' form.

Q:  Why are Rachel's eyes all red like that?

A:  Probably because she .....................  on her Extended Essay for hours, staring at her laptop.

If Rachel's eyes are still so red, the work with the laptop must be recent and linked to the present, so requiring the 'have + ing' form.

Q:  Why was Giovanni in such a bad mood when he arrived last night? He's usually so happy.

A:  Well, he told me that he ...... a serious row with his parents.

The correct answer 'had been having' is to suggest that the row had happened just before he arrived.

"We ...... online for two hours when suddenly the server crashed - just as we were about to win!"

The emphasis on 'for two hours' makes 'had been playing' necessary - the continuous playing is stressed.

"I don't usually like football, but I ...... to seeing Barcelona play this weekend.

Notice the general condition "I don't usually like..." contrasted with the present continuous feeling of "I am looking forward..." now.

Q:  Why's your hair all wet?

A:  .........................

If the hair is still wet, the person must just have been swimming, recently - an action over a period of time, connected to the present.
