Degrees of certainty

  • /
  • Language System/
  • Language of Instruction/
  • Language of caution /
  • Degrees of certainty

It is clear that the certainty, accuracy and authority of a statement all depend on specific elements of language - the elements that we have grouped under the heading Language of Caution.

Let us now look in more detail at some prominent components of this sort of language.

** Why not use the Presentation mode button, first in the row at top left, to present the following ideas ?

Basic degrees of truth

We need to have some sense of a continuum of how certain statements may be. Such a continuum would look something like this:-

.

Degrees of certainty

.

is not ... not likely ... may ... likely ... is

<------------------------------------------------------------>

.

.

There are a range of adjectives to replace 'likely' in this equation - probable / possible / conceivable / feasible, etc - and the wider the student's vocabulary in this area, the better.

.

'Certainty' above refers to the subject itself, to presumably objective evidence ... but there is also the subjective factor which influences the certainty of a statement :-

.

OBJECTIVE

it is

it may be

it could be argued

?? HYPOTHETICAL ??

I could argue

I would argue

I think / believe

SUBJECTIVE

.

This diagram has been arranged on a vertical axis, since it should properly be imposed on the Degrees of Certainty diagram above - in order to give a frame by which to judge the reliability of a given statement.

Using modals

As will be seen from the Objective / Subjective diagram, much of the weight of qualifying a statement rests on the use of Modal forms of verbs :-

.

Modals

can ... could

may ... might

should ... ought to

would

.

.

In order to have a competent command of the Language of Caution, a student needs not only to be able to form modal structures of verbs effectively, but also to have a sound awareness of how the use of modals, and related vocabulary such as 'likely', affects the certainty, reliability and authority of a statement.

Labels for statements

But the quality of a statement is not just affected by how forms of verbs are used - there are a whole range of adjectives, adverbs and phrases which label or categorise whatever they are linked to. For instance :-

.

Generalizations, broad statements

.

all in all ... by and large ... on the whole ... commonly

as a rule ... for the most part ... broadly

generally ... in general ... overall

on average ... on balance

essentially ... in essence

ultimately

basically ... fundamentally

.

.

.

.

When a statement does not apply 100% ...

.

... mostly ... generally ... usually ... normally

almost ... virtually ... practically

fairly ... sufficiently ... considerably ... largely

  principally ... significantly ... primarily

.

.

.

When a statement applies 100%

.

only ... solely ... exclusively ... entirely

absolutely ... specifically ... totally

.

.

Distancing & qualifying

.

in a way ... in effect ... more or less

to some extent ... up to a point


ALSO - but only used in the middle of a sentence :

...almost... ...practically... ...virtually...

ALSO - begin with various verbs, or verb forms, like

I think... I would have thought... I suppose...

I guess... I would imagine...

.

"I don't know whether you want to know this, but ...... you shouldn't be wasting so much time playing online games."

The options 'seriously' and 'honestly' are not really appropriate because they are not the real point of the statement. But why is 'as far as I'm concerned' not appropriate? Because the speaker may have an opinion about what the friend is doing - but has no right to be directly concerned or involved in what the person does.

"It is not very ...... that they have got lost - my father has just installed a fantastic new GPS in the car, with alternative routes and everything..."
